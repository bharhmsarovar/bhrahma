Who is Lord Varaha?

Varaha is the third of the ten principal avatars (Dashavatara) of Vishnu in Hindu tradition. The name means boar, and the avatar takes the form of a giant cosmic boar. Within the Vaishnava tradition that reveres Vishnu as the supreme deity, each avatar is understood as a descent of the divine into the world to restore righteousness and protect creation.

Varaha stands among the earliest of Vishnu's earthly forms, appearing in the Vedic texts and expanding in narrative scope through the Puranas. The Varaha Purana and the Bhagavata Purana both dedicate significant passages to the Varaha episode, which is treated not simply as a story of physical rescue but as a cosmological and theological statement about the nature of the earth, the universe and the relationship between divinity and matter.

Iconographically, Varaha is depicted as a figure with a human body and the head of a boar, frequently shown carrying or lifting the figure of Bhudevi, the earth goddess. The four arms of the standard depiction hold the conch, the discus, the mace and a lotus — the same attributes as Vishnu — though the boar tusk and the form of Bhudevi are the identifying features. Temples dedicated to Varaha are found across India, but they are concentrated in areas with strong Vaishnava pilgrimage traditions.

The mythological story behind Varaha Temple

The principal myth connected to the Varaha avatar concerns the demon Hiranyaksha, who seized the earth and dragged her into the cosmic ocean at the beginning of a new cycle of creation. Vishnu, in the form of a boar, dived into the primordial waters and fought Hiranyaksha for one thousand years before defeating him and lifting the earth back to her rightful place. The myth is among the oldest in the Hindu textual tradition and appears in the Shatapatha Brahmana before its fuller development in the Puranas.

The Pushkar connection to Varaha is part of the broader sacred geography of Pushkar, which Hindu texts identify as a place where the divine and the earthly meet with unusual closeness. While this guide is not the authority on local religious tradition, Pushkar's three main sacred sites — the lake, the Brahma Temple and the Varaha Temple — are understood collectively by devotees as parts of a unified pilgrimage landscape. Visitors who wish to understand the specific stories associated with this temple are advised to ask a knowledgeable local priest or consult published accounts from the temple trust or Rajasthan Tourism rather than relying on general web summaries, which vary considerably.

The mythological traditions should be held separately from the historical record of the physical temple, which is a different kind of question and is addressed in the next section.

Temple history: what is documented and what is uncertain

Pushkar is an old pilgrimage town, and the presence of a Varaha shrine here is consistent with the town's long Vaishnava tradition. However, the precise age and chronology of the current Varaha Temple structure requires care, because accounts from secondary sources differ significantly and many repeat each other without citing primary material.

What can be said with reasonable confidence is that the tradition of worship at a Varaha site in Pushkar is of considerable antiquity. Some scholarly sources associate certain temple sites in Pushkar with the early medieval period, while others note that many structures across Rajasthan were rebuilt, extended or restored during the medieval and early modern periods following episodes of damage. The Archaeological Survey of India maintains records on protected monuments in Rajasthan, and Rajasthan Tourism's official publications on Pushkar are a more reliable starting point than informal travel writing if you wish to investigate the history further.

What visitors observe today is a working temple rather than a ruin, so the structure visible on any given visit may reflect multiple periods of construction and repair. This is normal and not a mark against the site's authenticity; most living temples across India are the product of sustained maintenance and renewal over centuries. If the history of the specific structure matters to you, the temple trust or the local priests are the people best placed to give an informed account.

Architecture and key features

Varaha Temple in Pushkar follows the architectural grammar found across many Hindu temples in Rajasthan, with a sanctum sanctorum (garbhagriha) at its core, a mandapa or hall for assembly, and a shikhara tower above the sanctum. The use of local stone, carved detailing on the exterior walls and panels, and the enclosed courtyard are consistent with temple-building traditions in this region.

The sanctum contains the principal image of Varaha. Temples dedicated to Varaha often feature the goddess Bhudevi either held in the arms of the deity or shown separately, and visitors to working Vaishnava temples such as this typically encounter the deity dressed in cloth garments and adorned with flowers during puja (ritual worship), so the stone image itself may not always be directly visible.

The exterior of the temple rewards careful looking. Stone carving in Rajasthani temples of the medieval and early modern periods frequently includes panels depicting scenes from the Puranas, images of other deities in the Vaishnava tradition, and decorative motifs derived from both religious and natural imagery. Photography rules at each temple vary, so observe posted notices and ask permission before photographing carvings, the deity or other worshippers.

The scale of Varaha Temple is modest compared to some of the large Rajasthani temples at Eklingji or Ranakpur, but its intimacy is part of its character and the experience of approaching the sanctum in a small working temple is often more immediate than in a large complex designed for mass movement.

Cultural and religious importance

Pushkar occupies a singular position in Hindu pilgrimage geography. It is one of the very few towns in India with a temple dedicated to Brahma, the creator deity, and this singularity has drawn pilgrims for centuries. The presence of Varaha Temple and other Vaishnava shrines alongside the Brahma Temple means that Pushkar offers devotees access to different traditions within Hinduism within a compact area. For those undertaking a pilgrimage circuit, this concentration is significant.

Varaha as a deity holds particular importance for those who see the avatar as representing not just physical protection but the sustaining of the created world. The earth herself, Bhudevi, is a goddess in her own right in Vaishnava theology, and Varaha's act of rescue is understood as the prerequisite for all subsequent life and civilisation. Visiting a temple dedicated to Varaha is, for devotees, an acknowledgement of this foundational act.

The cultural significance extends beyond strictly religious practice. The narratives associated with Varaha have influenced sculpture, painting and literature across South Asia for over a millennium, and the iconography of the boar-headed deity carrying the earth is one of the most immediately recognisable in Indian visual culture. Visitors with an interest in art history or cultural heritage rather than in religious practice will also find the site worth visiting for these reasons.

Pushkar's pilgrimage economy and its annual fair bring together people from many communities and traditions, and the town's temples have been visited by people of different beliefs for a very long time. Respectful behaviour and appropriate dress are expected regardless of one's own religious affiliation, and temple staff are accustomed to fielding questions from visitors who are unfamiliar with the customs involved.

Location and its surroundings

Varaha Temple sits within the pilgrimage core of Pushkar, in the area of lanes and temples that radiates from the lake. Pushkar is a compact town on a human scale, and most of the main temples and all of the principal ghats are reachable on foot from anywhere within the old town. The lake, with its 52 ghats, is the geographic and spiritual heart of Pushkar, and Varaha Temple is part of the constellation of sites that make a visit to Pushkar substantive rather than thin.

The surrounding lanes are characteristic of old pilgrimage towns in Rajasthan: narrow, stone-paved, lined with stalls selling flowers, religious items, clothes and food. During peak pilgrimage seasons, including around the Kartik Purnima full moon and the annual Pushkar Fair, these lanes become extremely busy and lanes near the temple can be slow to navigate. At quieter times, the same lanes are pleasant to walk and the pace of the town is more relaxed.

Other significant sites within a short radius include the Brahma Temple, the lakeside ghats, the Gurudwara and several smaller shrines. The relationship between these sites means that a morning spent moving between them on foot gives a good sense of Pushkar's religious landscape without requiring long distances or transport.

The visitor experience: what to expect

Arriving at Varaha Temple follows the pattern common to most working temples in Rajasthan. Remove footwear at the entrance, as signs and temple staff will indicate. Modest clothing is expected: clothing that covers the shoulders and knees is advisable for all visitors. Some temples keep a cloth available at the entrance for visitors who arrive without appropriate cover; check with the staff if you are unsure.

Inside, the puja schedule means that the atmosphere of the temple changes through the day. Morning and evening aarti (lamp-offering ceremony) are the busiest times, and the sounds and scents of active worship make these the most vivid times to visit. Midday is generally quieter. Visitors who are not participating in the puja are expected to wait to one side and observe without interfering with the ritual underway.

Entry rules at individual temples in Pushkar can differ. Some restrict entry to Hindus, others welcome all visitors. Look for posted notices at the entrance and ask the person at the door if the rule is not clear. This is a question the staff at any Pushkar temple are accustomed to answering.

A flower or coconut offering from one of the stalls near the temple is a common gesture by visiting pilgrims. Whether to do so is a personal decision, and no-one is obliged to purchase anything. If someone approaches you before you reach the temple with an offer of a ritual or a garland and then asks for money, you are free to politely decline. The same applies to guides who position themselves at temple entrances; if you want a guide, choose one yourself and agree the fee and scope before the tour begins.

Best time to visit Varaha Temple

Pushkar is a year-round destination, and Varaha Temple can be visited in any season. That said, the character of a visit changes significantly depending on when you come.

The winter months from November to February are the most popular for visitors, combining cooler temperatures — Pushkar sits at about 510 metres above sea level — with clear skies and the period that includes the Kartik Purnima full moon. If your visit coincides with Kartik Purnima, the temple will be busy and the atmosphere of the whole town intensifies considerably. Our Kartik Purnima guide covers what to expect on and around that day. The Pushkar Fair, held in November each year, draws very large crowds, and the period around it should be treated as a high-demand time for accommodation and transport.

Spring, from February to April, brings warmer temperatures and smaller crowds. This is a good time for those who want to visit the temple and the lake in a less pressured atmosphere. Summer (May to June) is hot by most visitors' standards, with temperatures regularly above 35 degrees Celsius, though the pilgrimage to Pushkar continues year-round and the town is never entirely without visitors.

The monsoon (July to September) brings humidity and intermittent rain but also transforms the landscape around Pushkar, and the lake often fills significantly during good monsoon years. The immediate period after the monsoon — September to November — sees the town fresh and green before the main winter season begins.

For the temple itself, the most vibrant times are the daily aarti timings, typically early morning and around sunset, and on festival days in the Hindu calendar. Ask at the temple or with Rajasthan Tourism's information resources for confirmed timings, as these vary and can change without advance notice on general websites.

Nearby attractions worth combining with your visit

Pushkar is compact enough that Varaha Temple sits naturally within a half-day or full-day circuit of the town's principal sites. The Brahma Temple, about 1 km from our stay and similarly close to Varaha Temple, is one of the very few temples in India dedicated to Brahma and is the most prominent pilgrimage site in Pushkar. The lake ghats — particularly Mahadeva Ghat, about 400 m from our stay — are the other essential experience, either for a holy dip or simply to sit by the water at dawn or dusk. The Gurudwara, about 300 m from our stay, offers a different kind of atmosphere and hospitality.

Further afield, the Pushkar Mela Ground, about 1.3 km from us, hosts the annual fair and is interesting even outside fair season for the sense of scale it gives the event. Peace Park, about 850 m from our stay, provides a quieter counterpoint to the lanes of the old town. Those with more time can arrange excursions to Ajmer, about 13 km away, which holds the Dargah Sharif of Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti, one of the most visited Sufi shrines in South Asia, and Anasagar Lake. Kishangarh, known for its marble market and the Kishangarh School of painting, is accessible from the same direction.

For those visiting Pushkar specifically to cover its religious landscape, the combination of Varaha Temple, the Brahma Temple, a ghat visit and an evening walk along the lake perimeter gives a well-rounded encounter with the town on a single day. Add the Gurudwara and the smaller shrines in the lanes for a fuller picture.

How to reach Varaha Temple, Pushkar

Pushkar has no commercial airport. The nearest airport with scheduled passenger services is Kishangarh Airport (officially Ajmer-Kishangarh Airport), about 35 km from Pushkar by road. Ajmer Junction railway station, about 13 km from Pushkar, is well connected to Delhi, Jaipur, Mumbai and other major cities. Buses run frequently between Ajmer and Pushkar. Taxis and auto-rickshaws are readily available for the Ajmer-to-Pushkar stretch, and most accommodation in Pushkar can help arrange transfers.

By road from Jaipur, Pushkar is approximately 145 km and takes around two and a half to three hours depending on traffic and route. From Delhi, the journey is around 400 km. Rajasthan Roadways operates buses on major routes, and private taxis can be arranged through Pushkar's many travel agents.

Within Pushkar, Varaha Temple is in the pilgrimage core of the old town and is most easily reached on foot from anywhere within the main area. Vehicles cannot enter the narrowest lanes. If you are staying at Brahma Sarovar Home Stay and Cafe in Choti Basti, the temple is about 1 km by our estimate; check the exact route in Google Maps and allow extra time during busy periods.

Parking for private vehicles is available at the periphery of the old town. Ask local information or your stay for current advice on parking areas, as these change with seasonal arrangements. Motorcycles and e-rickshaws can access some lanes that cars cannot.

A suggested one-day Pushkar sightseeing route

This is a loose outline for a first-time visitor rather than a rigid itinerary. Adjust the order and pace for your own group.

  1. Early morning: begin at Mahadeva Ghat or another lakeside ghat for sunrise or morning aarti at the water's edge. The atmosphere is at its quietest and most devotional in the early hours.
  2. Mid-morning: walk from the ghats toward the Brahma Temple. Arrive before the midday crowd. Spend time in the courtyard and main shrine. The lanes between the lake and the Brahma Temple are lined with stalls and are interesting in their own right.
  3. Late morning: from the Brahma Temple area, the lanes toward Varaha Temple pass through the older residential and pilgrimage quarter. Visit Varaha Temple before noon when the morning puja activity has settled and the afternoon heat has not yet peaked.
  4. Midday: return to your stay for a rest. This is the hottest and quietest part of the day, and building a break into the schedule is sensible, especially in the warmer months or if you have children or older relatives.
  5. Afternoon: use the cooler afternoon hours to explore the bazaar, visit the Gurudwara or walk the perimeter of Pushkar Lake.
  6. Evening: return to the ghats for the sunset aarti. The evening light on the lake is characteristic of Pushkar and is one of the experiences most visitors remember.

If you have a second day, Ajmer (Dargah Sharif and Anasagar Lake) makes a natural excursion. Some visitors also combine Pushkar with a visit to the Pushkar Mela Ground and the surrounding desert landscape. Our best time to visit Pushkar guide goes into more detail on seasonal planning.

Questions and answers

What is Varaha Temple in Pushkar?

Varaha Temple is a Hindu temple in Pushkar, Rajasthan, dedicated to Lord Varaha, the boar avatar of Vishnu. It is one of Pushkar's older temples and is considered an important site of pilgrimage within the town alongside the Brahma Temple and the sacred lake.

Who is Lord Varaha and why is the Varaha avatar significant?

Varaha is the third avatar of Vishnu in the Dashavatara, the sequence of ten principal incarnations. In Hindu mythology, Varaha took the form of a cosmic boar and rescued the earth, personified as the goddess Bhudevi, from the demon Hiranyaksha by lifting her from the primordial ocean. The avatar is associated with protection, sustenance and the restoration of cosmic order.

When was Varaha Temple in Pushkar built?

The original temple at this site is believed to be very old, with some accounts attributing its origins to the early medieval period. The present structure reflects later periods of construction and renovation. For verified historical dates, consult the Archaeological Survey of India or Rajasthan Tourism's published resources, as local accounts vary.

What are the timings of Varaha Temple, Pushkar?

Temple timings in Pushkar vary by season and festival, and can change without notice. We recommend checking with the temple directly or with Rajasthan Tourism's official resources before planning your visit. Do not rely on third-party websites for current timings.

Can visitors of all faiths enter Varaha Temple?
